KPIs & Business Analysis

Every analysis starts with a decision someone has to make. Before touching data, I pin down the question, the metric that answers it, and what “good” looks like.

Where I Have Applied This

  • ZettaByte Plus: partnered with stakeholders to translate operational requirements into technical product features, then diagnosed process bottlenecks and streamlined backend workflows, improving backend efficiency by 30%.
  • WebClox: trace operational bottlenecks to the driver behind them, recommend the workflow fix, and track whether it worked on the KPI dashboards I build.

  1. Clarify the decision and its owner

    Stakeholder interviews to capture the business question, the decision it informs, who owns it and how often it is made. The output is a one-page problem statement with scope, assumptions and success criteria that everyone signs off on.

    • Stakeholder interviews
    • Problem statement
    • Scope & assumptions
    • Success criteria

  2. Map the process and write testable requirements

    An as-is / to-be swimlane map shows where work waits and who owns each hand-off. Requirements are written as user stories with acceptance criteria (“As a regional manager, I want late orders flagged daily so that I can reassign them before the SLA is breached”, checked with Given / When / Then), prioritized with MoSCoW, and signed off in UAT against those same criteria.

    • As-is / to-be process map
    • User stories
    • Acceptance criteria
    • MoSCoW
    • UAT

  3. Build a KPI driver tree

    I decompose the headline metric into the drivers that move it, so every number has a lever: Revenue = Orders × Average Order Value, and Orders = Active Customers × Orders per Customer. When the top-line KPI moves, the tree shows which driver moved it.

    Illustrative example: decomposing a 12% quarter-over-quarter revenue decline

    Revenue                    -12.0%   (0.850 x 1.035)
|- Orders                  -15.0%   (0.900 x 0.944)
|  |- Active customers      -10.0%
|  `- Orders per customer    -5.6%
`- Average order value      +3.5%

Read-out: pricing is not the problem (AOV rose).
The decline is volume, mostly lost customers, so the
next questions are about retention, not discounts.
    • Driver trees
    • North-star metric
    • Volume vs. price split

  4. Define every KPI precisely

    Each KPI gets a written formula, grain, filters, data source, owner, refresh frequency and target, kept in a KPI dictionary so finance, operations and the dashboard all report the same number. For example, repeat-customer rate = customers with 2+ orders in the quarter ÷ customers with 1+ orders, excluding canceled orders. I pair lagging indicators (revenue, churn) with leading ones (lead response time, pipeline coverage) that give an early warning.

    • KPI dictionary
    • Leading vs. lagging indicators
    • SMART targets

  5. Give every number context

    A KPI on its own means little. I report it against target, against the prior period (MoM, QoQ, YoY) and against a comparable segment, and I use rates and per-unit metrics (per customer, per order) rather than raw counts whenever volumes differ.

    • Baseline
    • Variance to target
    • Period-over-period
    • Normalized metrics

  6. Diagnose, size, recommend

    I drill down the driver tree and use Pareto (80/20) and 5 Whys to isolate the root cause, then size the impact in money or hours so the fix can be prioritized. Every recommendation names an owner, an action, and the KPI that will show whether it worked.

    • Root-cause analysis
    • Pareto analysis
    • Impact sizing
    • Action plan

  7. Lead with the answer

    I write findings top-down: the recommendation first, then the two or three reasons, then the evidence. Situation–Complication–Resolution keeps it to one slide. From my New Wheels analysis: Situation, revenue declined 39% across the quarters analyzed; Complication, average shipping time rose 60%, from 28 to 45 days, over the same period; Resolution, commit to a 25-day shipping SLA and track shipping days and customer ratings every week.

    • Pyramid principle
    • SCR
    • Executive summary

SQL

I write SQL that someone else can read, rerun and trust: understand the grain first, validate before aggregating, and build complex logic in small, testable steps.

Where I Have Applied This

New Wheels, revenue & operations analysis: queried 1,000 orders from 984 customers across 49 U.S. states to track quarterly revenue, orders and ratings. Traced a 39% revenue decline to a 60% rise in shipping time (28 to 45 days) and proposed a 25-day shipping SLA.

  1. Learn the schema and the grain

    Map the tables, primary and foreign keys, and what one row represents (an order, an order line, a customer). Knowing the grain is what prevents a join from fanning out and silently double-counting revenue.

    • ER diagram
    • Primary / foreign keys
    • Grain
    • Join cardinality

  2. Validate before analyzing

    Row counts; COUNT(*) against COUNT(DISTINCT key), remembering that the distinct count ignores NULLs, plus GROUP BY key HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 to list the duplicates; NULL profiling; minimum and maximum dates; and orphan checks such as LEFT JOIN customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id WHERE c.customer_id IS NULL. Totals are reconciled against the source before any insight is shared.

    • Data profiling
    • Duplicate checks
    • NULL handling
    • Reconciliation

  3. Build in readable steps with CTEs

    Each CTE does one job (filter, join, aggregate, rank) and can be tested on its own. I filter early, select only the columns I need, and name outputs in business language rather than database shorthand.

    • CTEs
    • Subqueries
    • CASE WHEN
    • Clear naming

  4. Turn tables into KPIs

    GROUP BY / HAVING and conditional aggregation (SUM(CASE WHEN …)) for revenue, order volume, average order value (revenue ÷ COUNT(DISTINCT order_id), not an average of order lines), repeat-customer rate, ratings and shipping time (DATEDIFF(ship_date, order_date) in MySQL, ship_date - order_date in PostgreSQL).

    • Aggregations
    • Conditional aggregation
    • Date functions
    • KPI queries

  5. Use window functions for trends and ranking

    LAG / LEAD for quarter-over-quarter change; SUM() OVER (ORDER BY … ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) for running totals; RANK / DENSE_RANK with PARTITION BY for top-N per group (ROW_NUMBER when exactly N rows are needed, because ties can return more); ROW_NUMBER to de-duplicate to the latest record; and NTILE for RFM-style segmentation.

    • LAG / LEAD
    • Running totals
    • RANK / DENSE_RANK
    • ROW_NUMBER
    • NTILE

  6. Drill down to the root cause

    When a KPI moves, I decompose it through its driver tree and slice by time, region, product and customer segment until the change is isolated to one driver the business can act on, and I put that driver in the same query as the symptom (see the example below).

    • Root-cause analysis
    • Segmentation
    • Cohort analysis

  7. Keep it fast and correct

    Index join and filter keys, keep predicates sargable (no functions wrapped around indexed columns), and read the EXPLAIN plan on slow queries. I avoid the classic NULL traps: NOT EXISTS instead of NOT IN when the subquery can return NULLs, remembering that COUNT(column) and AVG skip NULLs, and 100.0 rather than 100 to avoid integer division. Assumptions are documented alongside the query.

    • EXPLAIN plans
    • Indexing
    • NULL semantics
    • Documentation

Example (MySQL 8): quarterly KPIs with the driver (shipping time) next to the symptom (revenue)

-- Data covers one year; for multi-year data, group
-- and order by order_year, quarter_number instead.
-- rating_score: customer feedback mapped to 1-5.
WITH quarterly AS (
    SELECT quarter_number,
           SUM(quantity * vehicle_price) AS revenue,
           COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)      AS orders,
           AVG(DATEDIFF(ship_date, order_date))
                                         AS avg_ship_days,
           AVG(rating_score)             AS avg_rating
    FROM   orders
    GROUP  BY quarter_number
),
with_prev AS (
    SELECT q.*,
           LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY quarter_number)
               AS prev_revenue
    FROM   quarterly q
)
SELECT quarter_number,
       revenue,
       orders,
       ROUND(avg_ship_days, 1) AS avg_ship_days,
       ROUND(avg_rating, 2)    AS avg_rating,
       ROUND(100.0 * (revenue - prev_revenue)
             / NULLIF(prev_revenue, 0), 1)
                               AS revenue_qoq_pct
FROM   with_prev
ORDER  BY quarter_number;

Machine Learning

My modeling workflow is built to answer one question honestly: will this model lead to a better decision than the business makes today? That takes a clear target, a baseline, leakage-free validation, and a metric chosen by the cost of being wrong.

Where I Have Applied This

  • INN Hotels, booking cancellations: 36,275 bookings with a 32.8% cancellation rate, so always predicting “not canceled” already scores 67.2% accuracy. Compared logistic regression with decision trees; a pruned tree reached 83% test accuracy against that 67.2% baseline and surfaced lead time, room price and special requests as the key drivers, leading to a recommendation for automated reminder emails.
  • EasyVisa, visa approval prediction: 25,480 applications, roughly two certified for every one denied. Applied SMOTE to the training data only, benchmarked five tree-based models and selected Gradient Boosting on F1 (0.61); education, wage and job experience emerged as the main drivers of approval.
  • AllLife Bank, customer segmentation: 660 credit-card customers grouped into three segments with K-Means (k chosen from the elbow curve and silhouette scores) and cross-checked with hierarchical clustering, leading to digital onboarding for branch-reliant customers and self-service channels for a segment averaging 6.9 support calls.

  1. Turn the business question into an ML problem

    Define the target variable and the unit of prediction (one row = one booking, one applicant, one customer). A continuous target means regression (price, demand); a categorical outcome means classification (canceled or not, certified or denied); no label means clustering for segmentation. I check the class balance on day one because it changes every later choice.

    • Target variable
    • Regression
    • Classification
    • Clustering
    • Class balance

  2. Audit and clean the data

    Profile data types, duplicates and missing values. Impute skewed numeric columns with the median and categorical ones with the mode or an explicit “Unknown” level (the imputers are fitted inside the pipeline, on training data only). Fix impossible values, such as negative employee counts, rather than silently dropping rows, and convert mixed units before comparing (hourly, weekly and monthly wages annualized to one yearly figure). Values that look odd but are real, such as zero-priced complimentary bookings, are kept and flagged; outliers that are data errors become missing, and genuine extremes are capped or log-transformed only when they distort a scale-sensitive model.

    • Missing-value imputation
    • Duplicate removal
    • Unit standardization
    • Outlier treatment

  3. Explore with the target in mind

    Univariate distributions and skew, bivariate views of each feature against the target (for example, cancellation rate by lead-time band), and correlation heatmaps to spot pairwise collinearity. EDA produces hypotheses that the model, or a statistical test, then confirms or rejects.

    • EDA
    • Matplotlib / Seaborn
    • Correlation heatmap
    • Hypothesis framing

  4. Engineer features

    Create features that encode business logic: total stay length, total guests, arrival month and weekday, price per person, tenure bands. One-hot encode nominal variables (dropping one level for linear models), ordinal-encode ordered ones such as education, log-transform skewed variables such as wage or price, and scale features for distance- and gradient-based models (K-Means, logistic regression) but not for trees. For linear and logistic models I check VIF and drop the highest-VIF feature one at a time, recomputing until all are below about 5.

    • Feature creation
    • One-hot & ordinal encoding
    • Log transform
    • Scaling
    • VIF

  5. Set a baseline before any model

    A majority-class DummyClassifier for classification, and the median or last period’s value for regression. Every result is reported next to that baseline: 83% accuracy means little when always answering “no” already scores 67%.

    • DummyClassifier
    • Naive baseline
    • Lift over baseline

  6. Split without leakage

    A stratified train/test split (70/30), or train/validation/test (60/20/20) when I tune without cross-validation, keeps the class ratio the same in every set. When the model will score future records, I split by time instead and drop any feature that would not be known at prediction time. Every preprocessing step (imputer, encoder, scaler) is fitted on training data only, and SMOTE resamples training data only, inside an imblearn pipeline, so the test set stays genuinely unseen.

    • Stratified split
    • Time-based split
    • Pipeline
    • Leakage prevention

  7. Handle class imbalance deliberately

    When the minority class is the one that matters (cancellations, denials, churners), I compare class_weight="balanced", SMOTE (SMOTENC when features are categorical) and random undersampling, always applied to training folds only, and judge them on F1 and recall rather than accuracy.

    • SMOTE / SMOTENC
    • Class weights
    • Undersampling

  8. Start simple, then earn complexity

    I begin with an interpretable model (linear or logistic regression, a shallow decision tree) and then try ensembles: Random Forest (bagging, which lowers variance) and Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost or XGBoost (boosting, which lowers bias). For regression, linear models give coefficients the business can act on and can extrapolate; tree ensembles capture interactions and non-linear effects but cannot predict beyond the range they were trained on. A complex model has to beat the simpler one by enough to justify the explainability it gives up.

    • Logistic Regression
    • Decision Tree
    • Random Forest
    • Gradient Boosting
    • XGBoost

  9. Tune and validate

    Stratified 5-fold cross-validation with GridSearchCV or RandomizedSearchCV over depth, minimum samples per leaf, number of estimators and learning rate; cost-complexity pruning (ccp_alpha) for decision trees. I compare training and cross-validated scores side by side to catch overfitting, and score the held-out test set only once, on the final model.

    • Stratified K-Fold
    • GridSearchCV
    • Cost-complexity pruning
    • Overfitting checks

  10. Set the decision threshold by cost

    The default 0.5 cut-off assumes both errors cost the same. I act on a case when probability × value saved by acting exceeds the cost of acting. If a ₹40 reminder email saves about 1 in 10 at-risk cancellations of a ₹4,000 room-night, it is worth ₹400 per real cancellation, so the threshold is 40 ÷ 400 = 0.1 and most at-risk bookings get a reminder. The threshold is chosen on out-of-fold predictions, never the test set, and probabilities are checked for calibration (reliability curve, Brier score) because SMOTE and class weights distort them.

    • Cost-based threshold
    • Precision–recall trade-off
    • Calibration
    • Brier score

  11. Explain it and recommend

    Permutation importance on held-out data (impurity-based importance only as a first look, since it favors high-cardinality features), logistic-regression coefficients converted to odds ratios with exp(β), and a confusion matrix translated into business terms: how many at-risk cases the model catches, and how many good customers it wrongly flags. The deliverable is a recommendation, not a score.

    • Permutation importance
    • Odds ratios
    • Confusion matrix
    • Business recommendation

  12. Document it and plan monitoring

    Every model ships with a one-page model card (purpose, data, metrics against the baseline, limitations) and a data dictionary. If the model goes live, I would track input drift with PSI (below 0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 watch, above 0.25 investigate), track live precision and recall as outcomes arrive, and retrain on a schedule or when the agreed metric floor is breached.

    • Model card
    • Data dictionary
    • Drift monitoring (PSI)
    • Retraining trigger

Choosing the Model

Problem Where I start When I move up
Regression A naive baseline (the median), then Linear Regression, checking linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals (for valid inference) and VIF Ridge for collinear predictors, Lasso or Elastic Net for feature selection; Random Forest or Gradient Boosting Regressor for non-linear effects and interactions, remembering trees cannot extrapolate
Classification A majority-class baseline, then Logistic Regression for odds ratios, or a pruned Decision Tree for rules stakeholders can follow Random Forest when a single tree overfits (high variance); Gradient Boosting / XGBoost when bias remains and predictive power matters most
Segmentation K-Means on scaled features, with k chosen by the elbow method and silhouette score Hierarchical clustering (Ward linkage, dendrogram, cophenetic correlation) to validate the structure; every cluster is profiled before it is named

Choosing the Metric

Metric I use it when Typical case
Accuracy Classes are balanced and both errors cost the same Reported next to the majority-class baseline, never the only selection criterion
Precision False positives are expensive Sending manual reviews or retention offers to cases that did not need them
Recall False negatives are expensive Missing a booking about to cancel, a customer about to churn, or a fraudulent transaction
F1 score Both errors matter and the classes are imbalanced Comparing models for cancellations or denials; F1 weights both errors equally, so with known costs I tune the threshold as well
ROC-AUC / PR-AUC Comparing models independently of the threshold; PR-AUC under heavy imbalance Shortlisting models before threshold tuning
Lift / Gains Only the top of the ranked list will be acted on Targeting the 10% of customers most likely to churn with a retention offer
Brier score Predicted probabilities feed a cost or revenue calculation Checking that resampling has not distorted probabilities
RMSE / MAE / MAPE Regression: RMSE penalizes large misses, MAE gives the typical miss (both in business units), MAPE gives a percentage error but breaks down when actuals are zero or near zero Forecasting price or demand
R² / Adjusted R² Explaining variance in linear models; adjusted R² penalizes features that add nothing Linear regression diagnostics
Silhouette / Elbow Choosing the number of clusters and checking how well they separate Customer segmentation

Illustrative pipeline (scikit-learn + imbalanced-learn): resampling and encoding happen inside cross-validation, so no fold sees synthetic or test data

from imblearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from imblearn.over_sampling import SMOTENC
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import (
    GridSearchCV, StratifiedKFold, train_test_split)

y = (df["case_status"] == "Denied").astype(int)  # minority = 1
X = df.drop(columns=["case_id", "case_status"])
cat_cols = list(X.select_dtypes("object").columns)
cat_idx = [X.columns.get_loc(c) for c in cat_cols]

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=42)

pipe = Pipeline([
    # resamples training folds only; keeps categories valid
    ("smote", SMOTENC(categorical_features=cat_idx,
                      random_state=42)),
    ("prep", ColumnTransformer(
        [("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"),
          cat_cols)],
        remainder="passthrough")),  # trees need no scaling
    ("model", GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=42)),
])

grid = GridSearchCV(
    pipe,
    {"model__n_estimators": [100, 200],
     "model__max_depth": [2, 3]},
    scoring="f1",
    cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42))
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)  # test set is scored once, at the end

Statistics & A/B Testing

Models predict; experiments prove. When a recommendation changes how the business operates, I want evidence that the change, not the season or the sample, moved the KPI.

Example Design

How I would validate the New Wheels 25-day shipping SLA: apply it to a randomly chosen half of eligible orders for a full quarter, with average customer rating as the primary metric and repeat-purchase rate as a guardrail; size the test to detect a 0.2-point change in rating at 80% power; and compare the groups with Welch’s t-test and a 95% confidence interval.

  1. Frame the hypothesis and the metric

    State H0 and H1 in business terms, pick one primary metric (conversion, average rating, revenue per customer), add guardrail metrics that must not get worse, and agree on the minimum detectable effect: the smallest change worth acting on.

    • H0 / H1
    • Primary & guardrail metrics
    • Minimum detectable effect

  2. Design the experiment

    Choose the randomization unit (customer, order, store) so the same customer never sees both versions, run a power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.8) to set the sample size, and run for full weekly cycles. No peeking: the stopping rule is fixed before launch.

    • Randomization
    • Power analysis
    • Sample size
    • Fixed stopping rule

  3. Match the test to the data

    Welch’s t-test by default for means, so equal variances are not assumed; a chi-square test or two-proportion z-test for conversion rates; Mann-Whitney U when samples are small and clearly non-normal (Q-Q plot, Shapiro-Wilk); and for three or more groups, ANOVA, or Welch’s ANOVA when Levene’s test shows unequal variances.

    • Welch’s t-test
    • Chi-square
    • Two-proportion z-test
    • Mann-Whitney U
    • ANOVA

  4. Check validity before reading results

    A sample-ratio mismatch check (a chi-square test on group sizes) catches broken randomization. When several metrics or segments are tested, a Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg correction keeps false positives in check.

    • Sample-ratio mismatch
    • Multiple-testing correction

  5. Report the effect size, not just the p-value

    p < 0.05 says a difference this large would be unlikely if there were truly no effect; it does not say the effect matters. I report the lift with a 95% confidence interval and compare it with the minimum detectable effect, so the decision rests on practical significance.

    • Effect size
    • Confidence interval
    • Practical significance

  6. When a randomized test is not possible

    I compare before and after against a control group that did not get the change (difference-in-differences), or use a matched comparison, and state clearly that this evidence is weaker than a randomized test.

    • Pre/post with control
    • Difference-in-differences
    • Matched comparison

Example (Python): sample size, then the test that fits the metric

from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestIndPower
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

# Orders per group to detect a 0.2-point change in average
# rating (standard deviation about 1.1), alpha 0.05, power 0.8
effect = 0.2 / 1.1  # Cohen's d
n_per_group = TTestIndPower().solve_power(
    effect_size=effect, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)

# Means: Welch's t-test (does not assume equal variances)
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(
    treatment, control, equal_var=False)

# Rates: two-proportion z-test on conversions
z_stat, p_value = proportions_ztest(
    [conversions_t, conversions_c], [n_t, n_c])

Power BI / Tableau

A dashboard has done its job if the person reading it knows what to do next within ten seconds. I design backwards from that decision.

Where I Have Applied This

  • WebClox (Power BI & Tableau): design interactive dashboards that track operational KPIs and give leadership live visibility into performance.
  • Car insurance claims (Tableau): built a six-part interactive story with calculated fields, parameters and an executive KPI dashboard. The analysis showed that SUVs and blue-collar drivers account for the highest claims, which led to a recommendation for risk-based premium pricing.

  1. Start from the audience and its decisions

    Executives need four to six headline KPIs with trend and variance to target; analysts need drill-down. Before building, I agree on KPI definitions, refresh frequency and access rules with the people who will use the report.

    • Requirements
    • KPI dictionary
    • Audience mapping

  2. Model the data as a star schema

    Fact tables at a clear grain, surrounded by dimension tables (Date, Customer, Product, Region) with one-to-many, single-direction relationships and a dedicated Date table for time intelligence. Cleaning and shaping happen upstream in Power Query: data types, unpivoting, merges and appends, de-duplication.

    • Star schema
    • Fact & dimension tables
    • Date table
    • Power Query (M)

  3. Write measures, not hard-coded numbers

    Power BI: DAX measures built on CALCULATE and filter context, DIVIDE for safe ratios, time intelligence (TOTALYTD, SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR, DATEADD) and variables for readability. Tableau: calculated fields, LOD expressions (FIXED, INCLUDE, EXCLUDE) for metrics at a different level of detail than the view, table calculations for running totals and percent of total, and parameters for what-if analysis and metric switching.

    • DAX
    • CALCULATE
    • Time intelligence
    • LOD expressions
    • Parameters

  4. Lay it out for a ten-second read

    KPI cards with variance to target and to the prior period across the top, the trend in the middle, breakdowns and detail below, following the natural Z-pattern. Line charts for trends, sorted bars for comparisons, no 3-D and no pie with more than a few slices, and one accent color reserved for what needs attention.

    • KPI cards
    • Visual hierarchy
    • Chart selection
    • Conditional formatting

  5. Add interaction with intent

    Slicers and filters for the questions users actually ask, drill-through and tooltip pages for detail on demand, bookmarks and Tableau story points to walk through a narrative, and dashboard actions to filter across sheets.

    • Drill-through
    • Report tooltips
    • Bookmarks
    • Dashboard actions
    • Story points

  6. Make it fast, secure and trusted

    Remove unused and high-cardinality columns, prefer Import mode with incremental refresh where the data allows, apply row-level security so each manager sees only their region, and reconcile every KPI against the source system before release.

    • Performance tuning
    • Incremental refresh
