Bonus Prediction for Insurance Agents

Predicts the monthly bonus a life insurer pays each field agent, then turns the prediction into decisions: which agents to enroll in engagement and upskilling programs, at thresholds set by program costs, and how large a trial must be to prove the programs work. The data were proven in SQL first, and every model is judged against three simple baselines.

  • 729 → 101agents per arm needed to detect a 5% lift once the prediction is used as a CUPED covariate

Approach

A life insurer wants to predict the monthly bonus it pays each field agent, understand what drives it, and decide which agents to enroll in engagement and upskilling programs.

  • Data. 4,520 customer records with policy, customer and agent fields. Checks in SQL proved the file is two loads of the same 2,260 customers (customer 7000000 + n is the twin of 7002260 + n), so every split keeps twins together.
  • Method. A portable SQL warehouse for validation, cleaning and KPIs; grouped cross-validation against three baselines; regularized regression, nearest neighbors, single trees and ensembles up to XGBoost, with the final model chosen by a one-standard-error rule; two classification flags with thresholds set by program costs; drift checks (PSI) and an experiment design.
  • Result. On 904 unseen records the final model, an average of XGBoost, random forest and gradient boosting, scores RMSE ₹537 and MAPE 10.1% (R² 0.872), against ₹635 for linear regression, ₹896 for the commission rule and ₹1,534 for the naive median. Total bonus lands within 0.40%. The engagement flag catches 96.6% of top-quartile records (F1 0.88; PR AUC 0.964 against 0.25 by chance).
  • Recommendation. Flag 1,401 records for engagement and 1,382 for upskilling. Add service quality to the scheme, since records with a complaint earn ₹4,109 against ₹4,065 without one. Test any change with the model’s prediction as a CUPED covariate, which cuts the trial from 729 to 101 agents per arm.
  • Deliverables. A SQL warehouse that runs on SQLite, PostgreSQL and MySQL, two executed notebooks, an Excel KPI dashboard (17 of 17 measures tested), a Power BI kit with 19 DAX measures and a page-by-page report spec, and a 56-page report.

Approach

How many tons of instant noodles should an FMCG company ship to each of its 25,000 warehouses, and where can advertising lift demand?

  • Data. A three-month snapshot of 25,000 warehouses with 24 fields (location, capacity, ownership, certification, staffing, incidents) and no sales, dates or costs. Shipped tons stand in for demand, and today’s rule, the average for each zone and capacity size, is rebuilt from the brief.
  • Method. A portable SQL data layer with 21 quality rules; leakage-safe pipelines; baselines, nine models, tuning and one-standard-error selection; a newsvendor safety buffer at a 75% service level (a short ton costs 3× an excess one); a fallback model for when storage issues are not yet reported; and a second model that flags high-volume warehouses.
  • Result. Tuned LightGBM misses by 852 tons (RMSE) on 5,000 unseen warehouses, against 11,655 for the current rule (R² 0.995). Shipping the forecast plus the buffer cuts the tons mismatch by 93% versus the rule, with a 99.4% fill rate against 78.2%. From planning inputs alone, the high-volume flag reaches average precision 0.714 against 0.25 by chance and catches 78% of high-volume warehouses (F1 0.65).
  • Recommendation. Ship the forecast plus a size-based buffer, keep the fallback model in the plan, and test advertising in a controlled geo experiment rather than shifting budget, because regions do not differ once warehouse traits are known. The pilot needs 850 warehouses per arm for 80% power.
  • Deliverables. A shipping plan for every warehouse, two executed notebooks, an Excel KPI dashboard, a Power BI kit with 17 DAX measures and their expected values, a stratified pilot assignment and a full report.

Approach

Can 30 measurements of cell nuclei from a needle-biopsy image flag malignant tumors reliably enough to support a clinician?

  • Data. Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic), UCI: 569 patients, 37% malignant, with 114 held out for a single final test.
  • Method. Eight models in scaled pipelines under five-fold cross-validation. A transparent L1 logistic regression, statistically tied with the SVM, with its threshold chosen on training folds to minimize cost when a missed cancer costs 10× an unnecessary biopsy. Then an audit: bootstrap and Wilson intervals, decision-curve analysis, tumor-size slices and a drift monitor that needs no labels.
  • Result. ROC-AUC 0.996 (95% CI 0.989–1.000). At the chosen threshold, recall is 97.6% (41 of 42 cancers) with F1 0.95 and specificity 95.8%, against a majority-class baseline that catches no cancers at 62.6% accuracy.
  • Recommendation. Use it as decision support that flags cases for biopsy or a second read, never as a diagnosis. It adds net benefit over “biopsy everyone” at every threshold from 0.02 to 0.60, but its misses are small tumors, so re-validate it before another lab’s measurements are used.

From the Start

  1. Eight models and an honest baseline 4 Nov 2026

    Eight models in scaled pipelines under five-fold cross-validation. Always saying “benign” scores 62.6%, so recall and ROC-AUC led, not accuracy.

  2. A transparent winner 4 Nov 2026

    The SVM’s 0.0002 lead was noise, so an L1 logistic regression won on interpretability, with a threshold that minimizes cost when a missed cancer costs 10× a false alarm.

  3. Phase 2: uncertainty and calibration 11 Nov 2026

    Bootstrap intervals turned “recall 97.6%” into “about 95–98%, 92–100% on 114 patients”; the probabilities are well calibrated (Brier 0.022); a 7-feature model keeps ROC-AUC 0.993.

  4. Phase 3: an independent audit 11 Nov 2026

    Decision-curve analysis, tumor-size slices with Wilson intervals, a model card, tests and a demo app.

  5. Label-free drift monitoring 12 Nov 2026

    An alarm that watches the inputs and the scores, because labels arrive long after predictions.

Removing the scaler cut SVM recall from 94.7% to 80.0%. Kept scaling inside every pipeline, fitted on the training folds only.

  • No age or ethnicity data, so performance across patient groups can’t be checked.
  • The 10:1 cost of a missed cancer against a false alarm is an assumption.

Fake News Detection

Separates fake from real news articles by their wording, then asks the harder question: does it still work on a source it has never seen? On its own it doesn’t (55%), so the project measures how much new-source data fixes that, and how to use the model as a review queue for fact-checkers.

  • 55% → 86%accuracy on a new source: trained on one source, then on both
  • 100%of the top 100 articles in the review queue were fake

Approach

Can word patterns separate fake from real news, and does that hold on a news source the model has never seen?

  • Data. The ISOT Fake News dataset (University of Victoria), 38,285 articles after cleaning, and 6,219 articles from the McIntire dataset as a second source.
  • Method. Found that the rule “contains (Reuters)” alone scores 99.5%, so stripped datelines, photo credits, links and handles and removed 6,613 duplicate or near-empty articles. Compared four linear models on TF-IDF, then tested on a time split and on the new source, with sentence embeddings, McNemar tests and a score-drift alarm.
  • Result. 98.9% accuracy in-domain (95% CI 98.7–99.2), with F1 0.988 and recall 98.5% on fake articles, against 52.3% for the majority class. On the new source accuracy falls to 55%; training on both sources lifts it to 86%.
  • Recommendation. Use it only as a triage signal that ranks articles for human fact-checkers, never for automatic takedowns, and watch score drift: on the new source the population stability index jumped to 2.05 (above 0.25 means act), an alarm that fires before a single article is labeled.

Approach

How far does each step from logistic regression to a CNN take accuracy, and does the model read handwriting it was not trained on?

  • Data. MNIST (54,000 training, 6,000 validation and 10,000 test images), with USPS postal digits (2,007) and Semeion forms from Italy (1,593) as outside tests.
  • Method. Logistic regression, then an MLP, a CNN, and a CNN with batch normalization and augmentation; robustness tests on shifted digits; ensembles and a compact model quantized to TensorFlow Lite; McNemar tests; and one shared preprocessing module, so training and serving cannot drift apart.
  • Result. 99.35% on the MNIST test set (95% CI 99.19–99.51), against 92.54% for logistic regression, and 98.99% on digits shifted by 4 pixels, where the CNN without augmentation drops to 86.76%. On new handwriting: USPS 97.5% and Semeion 88.6%, or 91.9% with the 148 KB model.
  • Recommendation. Ship the 148 KB model for phone use, keep MNIST-style preprocessing in the serving path (a naive resize costs 6 points on USPS and 16 on Semeion), and send predictions below 70% confidence to manual entry.

Approach

Classical computer vision or a trained detector, and can the plate be read as well as found?

  • Data. The Vehicle Registration Plates dataset (Roboflow, CC BY 4.0): 3,000 training images and a test set of 882 images with 902 plates from parking cameras, motorbikes and street photos.
  • Method. Haar-cascade and edge-contour baselines against YOLOv8n fine-tuned within a 1.5-hour CPU budget; EasyOCR on four enhanced crops with a plate-grammar corrector; then a near-duplicate leakage audit, image-level bootstrap intervals and an end-to-end success rate.
  • Result. YOLOv8n finds 97.6% of plates (95% CI 96.5–98.7) at 95.3% precision (F1 0.96, mAP50 0.982), against 29.2% recall for the Haar cascade. Reading is the bottleneck: about 16% of cars get an exactly correct plate, and 41% a reading within one character of a watch-list entry.
  • Recommendation. Detection is solved, so invest next in an OCR model trained on plate crops rather than a bigger detector, use a group-aware split so all copies of a photo stay on one side, and give each camera its own drift reference.

Approach
