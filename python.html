Regression & Classification · Insurance

Bonus Prediction for Insurance Agents

  • Python
  • SQL
  • scikit-learn
  • XGBoost
  • Random Forest
  • Gradient Boosting
  • Classification
  • Cost-Based Thresholds
  • CUPED
  • Excel
  • Power BI
Problem
A life insurer wants to predict the monthly bonus it pays each field agent, understand what drives it, and decide which agents to enroll in engagement and upskilling programs.
Data
4,520 customer records with policy, customer and agent fields. Checks in SQL proved the file is two loads of the same 2,260 customers (customer 7000000 + n is the twin of 7002260 + n), so every split keeps twins together.
Approach
A portable SQL warehouse for validation, cleaning and KPIs; grouped cross-validation against three baselines; regularized regression, nearest neighbors, single trees and ensembles up to XGBoost, with the final model chosen by a one-standard-error rule; two classification flags with thresholds set by program costs; drift checks (PSI) and an experiment design.
Result
On 904 unseen records the final model, an average of XGBoost, random forest and gradient boosting, scores RMSE ₹537 and MAPE 10.1% (R² 0.872), against ₹635 for linear regression, ₹896 for the commission rule and ₹1,534 for the naive median. Total bonus lands within 0.40%. The engagement flag catches 96.6% of top-quartile records (F1 0.88; PR AUC 0.964 against 0.25 by chance).
Recommendation
Flag 1,401 records for engagement and 1,382 for upskilling. Add service quality to the scheme, since records with a complaint earn ₹4,109 against ₹4,065 without one. Test any change with the model’s prediction as a CUPED covariate, which cuts the trial from 729 to 101 agents per arm.
Deliverables
A SQL warehouse that runs on SQLite, PostgreSQL and MySQL, two executed notebooks, an Excel KPI dashboard (17 of 17 measures tested), a Power BI kit with 19 DAX measures and a page-by-page report spec, and a 56-page report.

Forecasting & Inventory Planning · FMCG

FMCG Supply Chain Optimization

  • Python
  • SQL
  • LightGBM
  • XGBoost
  • Stacking
  • SHAP
  • Conformal Intervals
  • Newsvendor Model
  • Classification
  • Power Analysis
  • Excel
  • Power BI
Problem
How many tons of instant noodles should an FMCG company ship to each of its 25,000 warehouses, and where can advertising lift demand?
Data
A three-month snapshot of 25,000 warehouses with 24 fields (location, capacity, ownership, certification, staffing, incidents) and no sales, dates or costs. Shipped tons stand in for demand, and today’s rule, the average for each zone and capacity size, is rebuilt from the brief.
Approach
A portable SQL data layer with 21 quality rules; leakage-safe pipelines; baselines, nine models, tuning and one-standard-error selection; a newsvendor safety buffer at a 75% service level (a short ton costs 3× an excess one); a fallback model for when storage issues are not yet reported; and a second model that flags high-volume warehouses.
Result
Tuned LightGBM misses by 852 tons (RMSE) on 5,000 unseen warehouses, against 11,655 for the current rule (R² 0.995). Shipping the forecast plus the buffer cuts the tons mismatch by 93% versus the rule, with a 99.4% fill rate against 78.2%. From planning inputs alone, the high-volume flag reaches average precision 0.714 against 0.25 by chance and catches 78% of high-volume warehouses (F1 0.65).
Recommendation
Ship the forecast plus a size-based buffer, keep the fallback model in the plan, and test advertising in a controlled geo experiment rather than shifting budget, because regions do not differ once warehouse traits are known. The pilot needs 850 warehouses per arm for 80% power.
Deliverables
A shipping plan for every warehouse, two executed notebooks, an Excel KPI dashboard, a Power BI kit with 17 DAX measures and their expected values, a stratified pilot assignment and a full report.

Classification · Healthcare

Breast Cancer Diagnosis

  • Python
  • scikit-learn
  • Logistic Regression (L1)
  • Cross-Validation
  • Cost-Based Threshold
  • Decision Curve Analysis
  • Bootstrap Intervals
  • Drift Monitoring
Problem
Can 30 measurements of cell nuclei from a needle-biopsy image flag malignant tumors reliably enough to support a clinician?
Data
Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic), UCI: 569 patients, 37% malignant, with 114 held out for a single final test.
Approach
Eight models in scaled pipelines under five-fold cross-validation. A transparent L1 logistic regression, statistically tied with the SVM, with its threshold chosen on training folds to minimize cost when a missed cancer costs 10× an unnecessary biopsy. Then an audit: bootstrap and Wilson intervals, decision-curve analysis, tumor-size slices and a drift monitor that needs no labels.
Result
ROC-AUC 0.996 (95% CI 0.989–1.000). At the chosen threshold, recall is 97.6% (41 of 42 cancers) with F1 0.95 and specificity 95.8%, against a majority-class baseline that catches no cancers at 62.6% accuracy.
Recommendation
Use it as decision support that flags cases for biopsy or a second read, never as a diagnosis. It adds net benefit over “biopsy everyone” at every threshold from 0.02 to 0.60, but its misses are small tumors, so re-validate it before another lab’s measurements are used.

NLP Classification · Media

Fake News Detection

  • Python
  • NLP
  • TF-IDF
  • Linear SVM
  • Logistic Regression
  • Sentence Embeddings
  • McNemar Test
  • Drift Monitoring
Problem
Can word patterns separate fake from real news, and does that hold on a news source the model has never seen?
Data
The ISOT Fake News dataset (University of Victoria), 38,285 articles after cleaning, and 6,219 articles from the McIntire dataset as a second source.
Approach
Found that the rule “contains (Reuters)” alone scores 99.5%, so stripped datelines, photo credits, links and handles and removed 6,613 duplicate or near-empty articles. Compared four linear models on TF-IDF, then tested on a time split and on the new source, with sentence embeddings, McNemar tests and a score-drift alarm.
Result
98.9% accuracy in-domain (95% CI 98.7–99.2), with F1 0.988 and recall 98.5% on fake articles, against 52.3% for the majority class. On the new source accuracy falls to 55%; training on both sources lifts it to 86%.
Recommendation
Use it only as a triage signal that ranks articles for human fact-checkers, never for automatic takedowns, and watch score drift: on the new source the population stability index jumped to 2.05 (above 0.25 means act), an alarm that fires before a single article is labeled.

Computer Vision · Document Processing

Handwritten Digit Recognition

  • Python
  • TensorFlow / Keras
  • CNN
  • Batch Normalization
  • Data Augmentation
  • Ensembles
  • TensorFlow Lite
  • External Validation
Problem
How far does each step from logistic regression to a CNN take accuracy, and does the model read handwriting it was not trained on?
Data
MNIST (54,000 training, 6,000 validation and 10,000 test images), with USPS postal digits (2,007) and Semeion forms from Italy (1,593) as outside tests.
Approach
Logistic regression, then an MLP, a CNN, and a CNN with batch normalization and augmentation; robustness tests on shifted digits; ensembles and a compact model quantized to TensorFlow Lite; McNemar tests; and one shared preprocessing module, so training and serving cannot drift apart.
Result
99.35% on the MNIST test set (95% CI 99.19–99.51), against 92.54% for logistic regression, and 98.99% on digits shifted by 4 pixels, where the CNN without augmentation drops to 86.76%. On new handwriting: USPS 97.5% and Semeion 88.6%, or 91.9% with the 148 KB model.
Recommendation
Ship the 148 KB model for phone use, keep MNIST-style preprocessing in the serving path (a naive resize costs 6 points on USPS and 16 on Semeion), and send predictions below 70% confidence to manual entry.

Object Detection & OCR · Transport

License Plate Detection and Reading

  • Python
  • YOLOv8
  • OpenCV
  • EasyOCR
  • Transfer Learning
  • IoU / mAP
  • Bootstrap Intervals
  • Drift Monitoring
Problem
Classical computer vision or a trained detector, and can the plate be read as well as found?
Data
The Vehicle Registration Plates dataset (Roboflow, CC BY 4.0): 3,000 training images and a test set of 882 images with 902 plates from parking cameras, motorbikes and street photos.
Approach
Haar-cascade and edge-contour baselines against YOLOv8n fine-tuned within a 1.5-hour CPU budget; EasyOCR on four enhanced crops with a plate-grammar corrector; then a near-duplicate leakage audit, image-level bootstrap intervals and an end-to-end success rate.
Result
YOLOv8n finds 97.6% of plates (95% CI 96.5–98.7) at 95.3% precision (F1 0.96, mAP50 0.982), against 29.2% recall for the Haar cascade. Reading is the bottleneck: about 16% of cars get an exactly correct plate, and 41% a reading within one character of a watch-list entry.
Recommendation
Detection is solved, so invest next in an OCR model trained on plate crops rather than a bigger detector, use a group-aware split so all copies of a photo stay on one side, and give each camera its own drift reference.

Medical Imaging · Education Only

COVID-19 Detection from Chest X-rays

  • Python
  • TensorFlow / Keras
  • MobileNetV2
  • Transfer Learning
  • Grad-CAM
  • Shortcut Probes
  • External Validation
  • Calibration
Problem
Can a CNN tell COVID-19, normal and pneumonia chest X-rays apart, and is it looking at the lungs?
Data
Chest X-ray (Covid-19 & Pneumonia), Kaggle: 4,372 training films and a 1,288-film test set (116 COVID-19), plus 201 adult films from another collection as an outside test.
Approach
A CNN from scratch, then MobileNetV2 frozen and fine-tuned, with class weights and a COVID-19 threshold chosen on validation data; shortcut probes (image metadata alone, lungs hidden); Grad-CAM; an outside test that breaks the confound; Wilson intervals and calibration.
Result
96.1% test accuracy and COVID-19 recall of 93.1% (95% CI 87.0–96.5) at 100% precision (F1 0.96). But image metadata alone scores 93.5%, and 86% of adult non-COVID pneumonia films from another collection were called COVID-19: the dataset ties the diagnosis to the source and the patients’ age.
Recommendation
Not deployable, and stated as such. A credible version needs patient-level splits, the same hospitals and age range for every class, and a test on a hospital that contributed no training data.

Time-Series Forecasting · Grocery Retail

Grocery Demand Forecasting

  • Python
  • pandas
  • statsmodels
  • Holt-Winters
  • SARIMA
  • Rolling-Origin Evaluation
  • Forecast Combination
  • MAPE / Bias
Problem
Planners need a reliable 12-month demand forecast and to know how far to trust it.
Data
US Census Bureau monthly grocery-store sales from January 1992 to August 2026 (416 months), with the BLS food-at-home price index, both from FRED.
Approach
Seasonal indices and naive benchmarks, a regression with month dummies, and Holt-Winters on nominal and inflation-adjusted sales, scored on a 24-month holdout. Then eight rolling forecast origins, SARIMA, a check of the 95% range, calendar effects and a gradient-boosting benchmark.
Result
On the holdout, combining two Holt-Winters models scored 1.18% MAPE (bias +0.09%), against 3.62% for the seasonal naive forecast. Across eight origins that winner ranked fifth, so the final method is Holt-Winters plus SARIMA: 1.57% average MAPE against 2.22% for gradient boosting, with its 95% range verified.
Recommendation
Plan on the forecast of $938.7 billion for the next 12 months (+2.4%), staff and stock for the December peak (+4.7%) and February trough (−7.7%), hold safety stock of about 2.6–4.5% of monthly demand, and retrain when bias exceeds ±2% for three months running.

Regression & Optimization · Marketing

Marketing-Mix Modeling and Budget Optimization

  • Python
  • statsmodels
  • SciPy
  • Multiple Regression
  • Interaction Terms
  • Cross-Validation
  • Constrained Optimization
  • Bootstrap
  • Adstock & Saturation
  • Power Analysis
Problem
With a fixed budget of about $200,900 per market, how should spend be split across TV, radio and newspaper?
Data
The Advertising dataset from An Introduction to Statistical Learning (200 markets), plus weekly marketing-mix data with published true parameters to test adstock and saturation.
Approach
Four regressions compared on five-fold cross-validated error, adding a TV × radio interaction and diminishing returns to TV; marginal return per channel; constrained optimization within the observed spend range. Then regression diagnostics, a bootstrap of the uplift, a profit-maximizing budget, an adstock-and-saturation model and a sample size for the market test.
Result
The best model cuts cross-validated error by 73% against a plain TV and radio regression (R² 0.993). At the current mix, $1,000 more radio returns about 211 units against 39 for TV, and reallocating the same budget lifts predicted sales by 40% (90% bootstrap interval 39.0–40.2%).
Recommendation
Phase out newspaper, raise radio toward $50k per market while holding TV near $150k, and confirm with a geo experiment before scaling: with a pre-period covariate, 24 markets per group can detect a 10% lift.

Exploratory Analysis · Automotive

Car Buyer Analysis (Austo)

  • Python
  • pandas
  • Matplotlib
  • Seaborn
  • Exploratory Data Analysis
  • Cross-Tabulation
  • Conditional Probability
  • Outlier Analysis
Problem
A car company wants to know who buys which kind of car (hatchback, sedan or SUV) and how much they spend, and to test claims its sales team makes, such as that salaried men are easier to sell an SUV than a sedan.
Data
1,581 buyers with 14 fields: age, gender, profession, marital status, education, dependents, personal and home loans, salary, partner’s job and salary, and the type and price of the car bought.
Approach
Fixed gender typos and missing values, explored distributions, outliers (IQR), correlations and group differences, then answered six business questions with cross-tabulations and conditional probabilities.
Result
Women chose SUVs in 52.6% of purchases against 9.9% for men, and paid more on average (47,705 against 32,416). A salaried buyer chooses a sedan with probability 44.2%, and the SUV claim fails: 13.4% of salaried men bought an SUV against 45.4% a sedan. A working partner made no real difference to the price paid (35,267 with one, 36,000 without).
Recommendation
Aim SUV campaigns at women, sell salaried men on sedans rather than SUVs, and don’t target buyers by partner income, which did not change what they spent.

Classification · Hospitality

Booking Cancellation Prediction (INN Hotels)

  • Python
  • pandas
  • scikit-learn
  • statsmodels
  • Logistic Regression
  • Decision Trees
  • Pruning
  • Threshold Tuning
Problem
INN Hotels, a hotel group in Portugal, loses revenue when bookings are canceled late. Which bookings are likely to cancel, and which policies would cut the losses?
Data
36,275 bookings from 2017 and 2018 with 19 fields, such as lead time, room price, market segment and special requests; 32.8% were canceled. A 70/30 split keeps 10,883 bookings for testing.
Approach
Exploratory analysis of cancellations by lead time, segment, price and month; logistic regression with multicollinearity and p-value checks and a threshold tuned on the ROC and precision-recall curves; then decision trees, unpruned, pre-pruned to depth 5 and cost-complexity pruned.
Result
Always predicting “not canceled” scores 67.2% on the test set. Logistic regression at a 0.42 threshold reaches 80% accuracy with F1 0.69 on cancellations. A depth-5 pre-pruned tree with 28 leaves reaches 82.3% (precision 0.82, recall 0.59, F1 0.69), and cost-complexity pruning lifts recall to 0.79 and F1 to 0.81 (87.7% accuracy), at the cost of a 1,774-leaf tree.
Recommendation
Send automated reconfirmation emails before arrival to high-risk bookings (lead times beyond about 151 days with no special requests), refund less on online and high-price cancellations, cap stays at five days outside the corporate segment, and start a loyalty program, since repeat guests rarely cancel.

Classification · Immigration

Visa Approval Prediction (EasyVisa)

  • Python
  • pandas
  • scikit-learn
  • SMOTE
  • Gradient Boosting
  • Random Forest
  • AdaBoost
  • Hyperparameter Tuning
  • F1 / Recall
Problem
The US Office of Foreign Labor Certification processed about 776,000 employer applications in 2016. Which visa applications are likely to be certified, and which applicant profiles drive the decision, so reviewers can shortlist faster?
Data
25,480 applications with ten applicant and employer fields: continent, education, job experience, training needs, employer size and age, region, prevailing wage and its unit, and full-time status. 66.8% were certified, so always predicting “certified” is right two times in three.
Approach
Fixed 33 negative employee counts and put every wage on a yearly scale; stratified 80/20 split. Compared decision tree, random forest, bagging, AdaBoost and gradient boosting with class weights, on SMOTE-oversampled and on undersampled training data, then tuned the leaders with randomized search and five-fold cross-validation, choosing by F1 on denied applications.
Result
Gradient boosting trained on SMOTE data scored F1 0.61 on denied applications (precision 0.56, recall 0.68) at 71.4% accuracy, against F1 0.56 for the same model without SMOTE and no denials caught by the majority-class baseline. Tuning raised accuracy to 74.1% but cut F1 to 0.56, so the untuned SMOTE model was kept.
Recommendation
Use the model as a pre-screen that sends low-probability applications to a second manual review, and guide employers toward the profiles that clear most often: postgraduate degrees (78.6–87.2% certified against 34.0% for high school), prior experience (74.5% against 56.1%) and yearly rather than hourly wages (69.9% against 34.6%).

Clustering · Banking

Customer Segmentation (AllLife Bank)

  • Python
  • pandas
  • scikit-learn
  • SciPy
  • K-Means
  • Hierarchical Clustering
  • Silhouette Score
  • Customer Segmentation
Problem
AllLife Bank wants to grow its credit-card base and improve service. How do its customers differ in spending power and in how they contact the bank, and how should marketing and service respond?
Data
660 credit-card customers with five fields: average credit limit, number of cards, and the number of branch visits, online visits and calls to the bank.
Approach
Scaled the features, then compared K-Means for k = 2 to 9 on silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz and Davies-Bouldin scores; cross-checked with hierarchical clustering (cophenetic correlation for each linkage, Ward dendrogram) and profiled each segment.
Result
Three segments: 386 branch-reliant customers (average credit limit 33,800, 3.5 branch visits); 50 premium digital customers (average limit 141,000, 8.7 cards, 10.9 online visits); and 224 phone-dependent customers with low limits (12,200) and 6.9 calls each. The silhouette score is 0.52 at k = 3, and Ward clustering puts 659 of the 660 customers in the same segments.
Recommendation
Offer premium cards and digital-first service to the 50 high-value customers, bring branch-reliant customers onto digital onboarding, and fix the service issues behind the phone-heavy segment with better self-service, since its 6.9 calls per customer are the main support cost.
